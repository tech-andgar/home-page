Silence Isn't a Barrier: My System for Redesigning Tech Leadership

November 6, 2025 4-minute read
Technology • Leadership • Culture
Inclusion • Tech Leadership • Deaf Professionals • Communication • Software Engineering • Accessibility

TL;DR: My deafness forced me to treat communication like an engineering problem. The result is a system (documentation, async, interpreters) that not only works for me but makes teams faster and more efficient. True inclusion isn’t an accommodation; it’s a system upgrade for everyone.

[Image: An abstract image representing communication networks or a clean, orderly architectural diagram.]

In any job interview, there are two conversations happening at once: the one spoken aloud, and the one that isn’t. As a Deaf professional, that second conversation is often about whether my abilities will be judged by my talent or by the logistics of my hearing.

I recently came across an article from DeafMETRIX titled “The Brutal Truth About Deaf Leadership: Incompetence or Systemic Erasure?”. It hit me hard. Not as a theory, but as a mirror of the strategies I’ve had to build to navigate the tech world. It helped me put a name to what I do by instinct, especially after a recent interview process.

The Interview: An Audit, Not an Exam Link to heading

The article makes a key point: the problem isn’t deafness, it’s that the environment isn’t set up to adapt. This changes the game. An interview is no longer just about me proving what I know; it’s about me auditing the company’s cultural “operating system.”

That’s why, before I agree to any call, I always ask about the communication culture. Do they thrive on constant, impromptu meetings, or do they value asynchronous communication and clear documentation?

This isn’t a logistical question. It’s a stress test. The answer tells me if the company is flexible, if it values clarity. And the truth is, a strong async culture is, by design, more inclusive and almost always more efficient for everyone.

The Paradox of Having to Be “Exceptional” Link to heading

DeafMETRIX talks about the “competence paradox”: the unspoken expectation that you have to be twice as good just to be considered half as competent.

In my last interview, after passing that initial filter, the technical deep-dive was intense. The feedback, thankfully, was incredibly good. They told me my experience far exceeded their expectations. It felt great, of course, but it was also a reminder of that paradox.

I can’t help but wonder: if my profile had been merely “good” instead of “exceptional,” would the inherent questions about communication logistics have weighed more heavily? It’s impossible to know, but it makes you think about the invisible bar that so many of us have to clear.

My System: Communication as an Engineering Problem Link to heading

Perhaps the most important thing I’ve learned is that managing my own accessibility is, in itself, a test of leadership. I don’t wait for people to figure things out for me. I present my system.

My approach is simple: “I’m a Deaf professional, and I treat communication like an engineering problem: with the right tools and process.”

My system has three pillars:

  1. Async by Default: The foundation of everything is written communication. Daily work flows through clear text and rigorous documentation.
  2. Interpreters for Sync: For important, planned meetings, we bring in professional interpreters. They are the bridge to high-fidelity communication, ensuring a fluency that real-time text sometimes can’t match.
  3. Clear Guidelines: I share resources, like the CNCF’s guides, so everyone knows how to make the collaboration work smoothly.

When I explained this in the interview, the company leader’s reaction was everything. He didn’t see it as a problem, but as a strength. He told me, “This tells me that for you, there are no problems, only solutions.”

In that moment, the conversation stopped being about my deafness. It became about what really matters in a startup: speed, efficiency, and risk mitigation. By presenting a system, I transformed a potential barrier into a demonstration of the very leadership skills they were looking for.

It’s Not an Accommodation, It’s a Redesign Link to heading

I was lucky to speak with leaders who saw beyond the obvious. But the experience reinforces the article’s core idea: the real challenge isn’t the individual, it’s the design of our work systems.

For other Deaf professionals, this means we have to be the architects of our own communication systems. And for companies, the lesson is even bigger. Think of it this way: ambiguous communication and a lack of documentation are essentially “communication debt.” Like technical debt, it gives you a false sense of speed at first, but it always, always, makes you slower and more costly in the future.

The principles of clear communication and intentional collaboration that benefit me are the same principles that build elite teams for everyone.

Silence isn’t a barrier. It’s an opportunity to communicate better.
